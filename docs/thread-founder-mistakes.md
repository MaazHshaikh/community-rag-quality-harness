# Biggest mistake you made in year one?

**Doc type:** Discussion thread
**Date:** 2026-09-18

**Gabriel Ashford** (founder, Lumenvault, backup software for architecture firms):
I'll start. I spent the first eight months building features nobody asked for. We had 22 features at launch and customers used 3 of them. If I could redo it, I'd ship the smallest useful version in two months and build only what paying customers requested. What's yours?

**Zainab Yusuf** (founder, Cindergate, maintenance tracking for property managers):
Not having a cofounder agreement in writing. My cofounder and I split equity 50/50 on a handshake. When he left after seven months, he still owned half the company because there was no vesting. It took four months and $18K in legal fees to sort out. Always use 4-year vesting with a 1-year cliff, even with your best friend.

**Henrik Dahl** (founder, Ploughline, route planning for rural delivery):
Ignoring my own health and calling it commitment. I worked 90-hour weeks for most of the first year, and my decisions got worse every month. I made two bad hires in that stretch purely because I was too tired to think. Now I cap myself at 55 hours and take Sundays fully off. The company grew faster after I started doing that.

**Mei-Lin Chow** (founder, Scribbleport, note-taking for courtrooms):
Waiting too long to talk about money with customers. I ran 40 discovery calls without once asking "would you pay for this, and how much?" Everyone said they loved the idea. When we launched at $60 per month, only 2 of those 40 people bought. Now I ask about price in the first call, every time.

**Gabriel Ashford:**
Mei-Lin, that one hurts because I did the same. Love for an idea and willingness to pay are different things.

**Zainab Yusuf:**
A good summary for anyone reading later: build less, put agreements in writing, protect your health, and ask about money early. Most of these cost almost nothing to avoid if you do them in the first month.
